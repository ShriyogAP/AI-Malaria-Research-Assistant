# Curated Malaria Research Knowledge Base

This is a small educational collection, not a complete clinical reference. Recheck official sources before presenting medical guidance.

## SOURCE: World Health Organization — Malaria Fact Sheet

URL: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malaria

Summary:

Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by Plasmodium parasites and transmitted mainly through bites from infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. It is preventable and curable, but symptoms can be mild or life-threatening.

Common early symptoms include fever, chills, and headache. Severe signs can include extreme fatigue, impaired consciousness, seizures, difficulty breathing, dark or bloody urine, jaundice, and abnormal bleeding. Severe symptoms require emergency care.

Early diagnosis and treatment are important. Suspected cases should be confirmed using parasite-based testing, such as microscopy or a rapid diagnostic test. Prevention includes reducing mosquito bites and using recommended prevention measures in consultation with healthcare professionals.

## SOURCE: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — About Malaria

URL: https://www.cdc.gov/malaria/about/index.html

Summary:

Malaria is caused by a parasite and most often spreads through the bite of an infected Anopheles mosquito. Symptoms can include fever or flu-like illness, chills, headache, muscle aches, tiredness, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.

If not treated quickly, malaria can become severe. Only a healthcare provider can diagnose malaria, and a laboratory test using a blood sample confirms infection.

Treatment decisions depend on factors including parasite species, location of infection, illness severity, age, and pregnancy status. Prevention may include mosquito-bite avoidance and discussing preventive medicines with a healthcare professional before travel to areas where malaria occurs.

## SOURCE: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — Testing for Malaria

URL: https://www.cdc.gov/malaria/testing/

Summary:

A laboratory test using a blood sample is needed to confirm malaria. Blood-smear microscopy can detect parasites and help identify the species. Rapid diagnostic tests can provide useful information but have limitations; healthcare professionals interpret results and may use additional testing.

People who develop symptoms after travel to an area where malaria occurs should seek prompt medical assessment. A chatbot or image-classification model is not a substitute for clinical testing.

## SOURCE: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — Symptoms of Malaria

URL: https://www.cdc.gov/malaria/symptoms/

Summary:

Malaria symptoms may begin a week or more after infection and, in some cases, can occur much later. Symptoms may include fever, chills, headache, muscle aches, tiredness, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.

Severe disease can cause anemia, jaundice, kidney failure, seizures, confusion, coma, or death. Symptoms alone cannot confirm malaria. People with possible exposure and compatible symptoms should seek medical care promptly.

## SOURCE: CNN Research Overview — Malaria Blood-Cell Image Classification

URL: https://lhncbc.nlm.nih.gov/LHC-publications/pubs/MalariaDataset.html

Summary:

Machine-learning research has explored microscopy images of thin blood smears to classify infected and uninfected red blood cells. A convolutional neural network (CNN) learns visual patterns from labeled training images.

Reliable evaluation requires careful dataset splitting, preventing leakage between training and test sets, reporting appropriate metrics, checking class balance, and validating on data from different sources.

A high score on one dataset does not prove clinical readiness. This prototype does not load a CNN model or classify images.
